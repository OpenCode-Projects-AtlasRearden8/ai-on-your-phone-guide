Chapter 6. Accessibility Extras

Bigger text, screen readers, hands-free, one-handed use, and low-energy routines. The AI features that help most when your eyes, hands, or attention need extra support.

The short version

Your phone's built-in accessibility features do more for "using AI comfortably" than any special app, and they're all free and already installed. This chapter is a short tour of the ones that matter, plus the AI habits that particularly help with low vision, shaky hands, chronic pain, and attention.

Making text bigger and clearer

  • iPhone: Settings → Accessibility → Display & Text Size → Larger Text. There's also Bold Text, and Reduce Contrast if white backgrounds feel glaring.
  • Android: Settings → Accessibility → Text size and display size. Also "High contrast text."

This enlarges everything system-wide, including AI chat apps. Chat apps that ignore giant-text settings are poorly built; a handful don't grow with the setting — the good ones do.

Screen readers work fine with AI apps

  • iPhone: VoiceOver (Settings → Accessibility → VoiceOver).
  • Android: TalkBack (Settings → Accessibility → TalkBack).

Modern AI chat apps are tested against both, so reading AI replies aloud on your phone is simply talking with the tool you already use. Tips:

  • Long AI replies read nicer with a two-finger swipe-down (VoiceOver's read-all gesture); on TalkBack the read-from-top gesture lives in the same gesture menus you already use.
  • Headphones make spoken AI replies genuinely usable in public without broadcasting.

The three unbeatable AI features for accessibility

1. Camera → "get answers" (mainstream and astonishing)

Modern AI chat apps can look at a photo and answer questions about it. For low vision this is quietly life-changing: photographing a stove dial and asking "what temperature is this set to?", reading a label, checking what a link says before tapping it. Look for the camera or image button in your AI app's input area, point the phone, and ask out loud. Free tiers include this today.

2. Voice (recap from Chapter 3, re-framed)

For hands that hurt, tremor, or are simply full — voice dictation + conversation mode mean you can use AI completely without typing. The one-tap voice button and spoken replies equal a hands-free assistant. This alone is reason enough to build the Chapter 3 habit.

3. Rewriting at a level and length you choose

Ask for the format you need, every time:

  • "Explain this like I have 20 seconds."
  • "Rewrite with short sentences and simple words."
  • "Give me just the steps, nothing else."

People with dyslexia, ADHD, brain fog, or second-language reading fatigue tell us this is the single most valuable thing AI does for them: the same information, rebuilt at the size you can absorb today. It never gets tired of repeating itself. Ask again at half the length and there's no sigh.

ADHD-friendly and low-energy routines

A few patterns that work well for attention (and honestly, for everyone):

  • One question per screen. Ask a single, narrow thing. Follow-ups feel lighter than composing the whole plan at once.
  • Say "give me the first step only." It stops the wall of text that gets saved-and-never-read. Ask for step 2 only after step 1 is done.
  • Voice notes to self. On a rough day, ramble out loud into a note app or (better) straight into the AI chat: "help me turn this voice-mumble into a tidy list of three things I have to do."
  • Same question, shorter brain: "Rewrite this for a day when I have no energy" is a legitimate prompt. It works.
  • Gentle fences: if scrolling away from the AI app is the problem ("I'll just ask one more"), turning notifications off works better than willpower. AI apps rarely have the slot-machine feeds social apps do — that's a quiet gift in their settings (Chapter 4).

One-handed and shaky-hands mode

  • Put the keyboard in one-handed mode when you're walking or carrying coffee. Gboard: long-press the keyboard icon → one-handed mode. iPhone: Settings → Accessibility → Touch → Reachability, then swipe down at the bottom of the screen.
  • Increase tap targets if taps feel small: iPhone has touch-adjustment options under Settings → Accessibility → Touch; Android has similar options under Settings → Accessibility. Bigger targets help anyone with tremor, pain, or a phone balanced on a forearm.
  • If tapping is hard, remember that voice is your friend — Chapter 3 covers the full hands-free path.

Quick-start checklist (10 minutes)

  1. Bump text size up two notches. Look at an AI chat app, keep or undo.
  2. Try camera-to-AI once in good light: point at any object in your home and ask what it is. This is the wow-moment feature — share it with someone.
  3. Ask for a simple "3 steps, short words" rewrite of something you find online (paste into the AI app without personal info — see chapter 4).
  4. Locate the voice mode button in your AI app and use it once.

Checkpoint

You should now be able to:

  • Turn up text size and know that chat apps will usually follow.
  • Do the one gesture you need for a screen reader, or say "I'll come back to this when I want it."
  • Name the three best AI accessibility moves — camera answers, voice, custom-format rewrites.
